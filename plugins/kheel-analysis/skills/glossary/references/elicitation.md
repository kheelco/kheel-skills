# Elicitation

## Asking a practitioner

One or two questions at a time, letting each answer shape the next. Never fire the whole list, and skip any question
the evidence already answers.

1. **What do you call this?** Their word, in the form they use it. Resist improving it.
2. **What is it, in a sentence, to someone who works here?** Aim for *a [kind of thing] that [what sets it apart]*.
   Push past "it's the thing in the system where…".
3. **What is it most often confused with?** Then: what is the line between them? This is usually the most useful
   question of all.
4. **Give me a real one.** A concrete instance a practitioner would recognise. A term nobody can give an example of is
   a term nobody has understood.
5. **Are there other words for it?** Do teams, regions or generations say it differently? Which word wins, and why?
6. **Does this word mean something else elsewhere in the domain?** *Account* in finance is not *Account* in access
   control. One word with two meanings is two terms, each qualified.
7. **What kind of thing is it here?** Someone who acts, a thing in its own right, something done, or a property of
   something else (and if so, of what)?
8. **How does it stand to its neighbours?** A type of something? A component of something, or something associated
   with it? How do you say it: what does one *do*, *have* or *need* of the other, or what do you call the other from
   this one's side (its *primary actor*, its *directors*)? Note how many, if they say.
9. **Must anything be true for it to count as one?** A candidate definitional rule; most candidates turn out to be
   contextual rules or system constraints instead.
10. **Is it the same everywhere in scope?** Across jurisdictions, entity forms, markets: does it exist, is it called
    the same, do the same rules apply? And does it still mean the same? If not, it is two terms.

Record each answer's source (who said it, or which document) so the entry can be marked attested.

## Harvesting from notes

Given interviews, transcripts or documents, harvest first and ask only about the gaps. Candidate terms are nouns and
noun phrases that:

- **recur** across speakers or documents;
- **get defined in passing**: "what we call a…", "that's just our word for…", "technically it's a…";
- **carry a capital letter** in the domain's own documents;
- **get argued about**: two people using it differently, or one correcting another;
- **would stop a newcomer**: the word that made you ask "what's one of those?";
- **compound**: an ordinary noun with a qualifier that changes its meaning (an *open enrolment window* is not a
  *window*).

Verbs (*lodge*, *ratify*, *place*) point to what is done, which belongs to activity modelling; harvest the nouns they
act on. A verb becomes a glossary candidate only when the domain also names the act as a noun of its own (*Lodgement*,
*Ratification*, *Placement*) and talks about it as a thing.

List the candidates, then triage: domain-specific meaning, ambiguous, or ordinary word in its ordinary sense. Only the
first two go in. A glossary that defines *report* and *user* has been padded, and padding hides the entries that
matter.

Everything harvested is at best **inferred** until a source states it: a definition assembled from three passing
mentions is the agent's synthesis, not the domain's statement. Mark it so, and confirm the ones that matter.

## Coined terms

Where the domain has a thing and no settled word for it, coin one, mark it coined in its source, and say why. That is
honest and common, but a glossary in which most terms are coined is describing a product, not a domain.
