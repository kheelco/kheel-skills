# Rules and variation

## Three kinds of rule knowledge

Candidate rules turn up constantly while defining terms. Sort each into one of three:

| Kind | What it is | Where it goes |
|---|---|---|
| **Definitional rule** | Part of what makes something an instance of the term. Without it, the term means something else. *A special resolution requires a threshold, higher than for an ordinary resolution, measured over a defined body of voters.* | On the term, under **Must hold**. |
| **Domain rule** | Governs the term's behaviour or validity in a particular context: a jurisdiction, an entity form, an organisation's own instrument. *In New Zealand the threshold is 75% of votes cast.* *This company's constitution sets 80%.* | Referenced from the term under **Governed by** (the rule or the instrument that sets it) and, where it differs by setting, under **Variation**. The glossary is not its authoritative home: the business rules analysis is. |
| **System constraint** | What a solution must do or never do. *The system must reject a resolution recorded without a vote count.* | Leaves the glossary for the constraints analysis. Note the handover in the entry's notes. |

All three are genuine domain knowledge; the sorting decides where each is owned, not whether it matters. Most
candidates are not definitional. A glossary heavy with **Must hold** has usually swallowed a policy manual or a
requirements list.

A definitional rule states a **shape** — what exists and what it is measured over — and a value only when the value is
itself part of the meaning everywhere in scope. Where a value differs by setting it is variation; where an
organisation's own instrument sets it, the glossary records that the instrument governs the term, not what it says.

### Routes

A **route** is an alternative way an activity or outcome can be achieved: a resolution passed at a meeting, or in
writing. A route is chosen per occasion; a setting is given by the context. So a route is never a dimension of
variation.

Record a route's requirements wherever the underlying rule belongs, as alternatives within it:

- a requirement that is part of the meaning goes under **Must hold** (*Must hold: passed by a vote at a meeting of
  those entitled, or by a written resolution signed by them*);
- a requirement set by a jurisdiction, an entity form or an instrument goes under **Governed by** or, where it differs
  by setting, under **Variation** (*by meeting, a majority of votes cast; by written resolution, a majority of all
  those entitled*).

Most route-specific thresholds are of the second kind.

## Variation

A term may vary across a **dimension** (jurisdiction, entity form, market), each with enumerated **settings** (*New
Zealand*, *Australia*; *company*, *incorporated society*). A setting is just one value of its dimension: nothing hangs
off it. Settings are what the glossary is read against, not a partition of the world: one company answers to several
jurisdictions for different purposes. Dimensions may compose (*listed issuer*, then market).

A term has **one definition, the same at every setting**. What may vary by setting are three **independent**
properties, any combination of which may differ:

| Property | Same | Differs |
|---|---|---|
| **Applicability** | The thing exists here | **Absent**: it does not exist at this setting |
| **Terminology** | Called the same | Called something else here: record the local word as a synonym scoped to the setting |
| **Rules** | Same domain rules | Different rules here: say which, or reference them |

Record only what differs, setting by setting; a term with no variation records none. An absent term needs no other
property at that setting. A dimension-wide summary is read off the settings, never written separately.

**Meaning does not vary; it splits.** If the definition would have to change at some setting, the word is naming a
different thing there. Make two terms, each qualified (*Director (NZ)* and *Director (US)*, or better, the words the
domain itself uses to tell them apart), each recorded as not to be confused with the other and each absent where the
other applies. If the meaning holds and only what it takes differs, it is one term whose rules vary.

The test is the definition: does it still describe the thing at this setting, word for word? A difference that can only
be written into the definition as a setting-specific clause is a difference in meaning; one that can be written as a
domain rule is a difference in rules. A definitional rule (**Must hold**) is part of the meaning, so it cannot vary
either: a definitional rule that differs by setting is either a domain rule or a sign the term should split.

**When to specialise.** Does the difference change what the thing *is*, or only what it *takes*? A solvency test and a
distributable-profits test ask different questions, so they are types of capital maintenance test. A special
resolution is the same kind of resolution everywhere with a different threshold, so it stays one term whose rules
vary.

### Example

The principle is what matters here: **a variation in requirements is not a variation in meaning.** The thresholds and
voting bases below illustrate the jurisdictions and contexts chosen for the example, summarised; they are not
properties of the term, and a real glossary takes them from its own sources.

*Special resolution* (Concept: the resolution, not the act of passing it) carries the definitional rule *requires a
threshold, higher than for an ordinary resolution, measured over a defined body of voters*. Under **Variation
(Jurisdiction)**:

- **New Zealand:** rules differ: 75% of the votes cast.
- **Australia:** rules differ: 75% of the votes cast by members entitled to vote; by written resolution (where
  allowed), every member entitled.

*Imputation credit* (Concept), under **Variation (Jurisdiction)**:

- **United States:** absent.

The term and its rules vary independently: the special resolution exists and means the same in every setting and only
what it takes moves; the imputation credit is simply not there in one.
