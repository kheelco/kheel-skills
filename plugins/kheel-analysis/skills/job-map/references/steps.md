# Steps

## The eight step types

Outcome-Driven Innovation names eight universal types of step. They are a **completeness framework for discovery**, a
set of questions to ask about any job, not eight mandatory stages.

| Type | The executor must… | Ask |
|---|---|---|
| **Define** | determine what needs to be done, considered or achieved | What do they have to work out before anything can start? |
| **Locate** | gather the inputs, information, resources or items required | What do they have to have to hand, and where does it come from? |
| **Prepare** | arrange or ready what is needed | What has to be set up, structured or made ready? |
| **Confirm** | verify readiness, assumptions, conditions or prerequisites | What do they check before committing? Who has to have agreed? |
| **Execute** | perform the central activity of the job | What is the act that, once done, cannot be undone cheaply? |
| **Monitor** | assess progress, state, performance or emerging conditions | What do they watch while it runs, and afterwards? |
| **Modify** | adjust execution or respond to deviations | What goes wrong, and what do they do about it? |
| **Conclude** | complete the job and establish the resulting state | How do they know it is finished, and what is left behind? |

A type may yield no step, one, or several. Order is loose: the types run roughly in sequence, but Monitor and Modify run
alongside Execute, and real jobs loop. Record steps in the order an executor would describe them, and say in a step's
notes where it runs alongside or repeats.

## Mapping a continuing job

A continuing job keeps something in a desired state indefinitely and is done through **episodes** (recorded on the job
in the topology), each with a trigger: event-driven (*incorporate a change in share ownership*), periodic (*verify the
accuracy of recorded ownership*) or exceptional (*correct an ownership record*). Map it as one job: the steps are what
the executor must accomplish to keep the state, and the episodes run through them (an event-driven episode passes
through Define, Confirm, Execute and Conclude; a periodic one through Monitor and Modify). Note on each step which
episodes pass through it.

The map is also the test of whether the job holds together, and it must be a real test, not a confirmation. If its
steps can be read as one coherent sequence, with the episodes running through it, the job stands. If they fall into
several **independent cycles**, each with its own beginning, end and outcomes and sharing few steps, stop and report
it to the topology: the episodes are constituent jobs of a higher job, each to be mapped on its own. Do not force
them into one map.

Two signs the test is being failed quietly:

- **An operating cycle.** Steps that read as the handling of one kind of event (receive a change → validate it →
  update → verify → correct) describe a workflow, not the executor's desired progress, and usually presuppose a
  particular mechanism. Restate each step as what the executor must accomplish, whatever started the episode.
- **Episodes with nowhere to go.** If the steps cannot be stated without presupposing the event that starts one
  episode, and the other episodes (a periodic check, where nothing has arrived) find no steps of their own, that is
  evidence the job is an overarching responsibility containing several jobs. Report it; do not bend the map to fit.

## Step rules

Each step says **what the executor must accomplish**, not how the current process or software makes them accomplish it.

| Prefer | Over |
|---|---|
| Determine which obligations apply to the company | Search the obligations register and select applicable obligations |
| Confirm that the transfer is permitted | Run the transfer restriction check |
| Record the completed transfer | Enter the transaction in the system |

- **A milestone, not a task.** Something that is either accomplished or not.
- **Verb + object**, in the executor's own language, the object a glossary noun, with a clarifier only where two steps
  would otherwise read alike.
- **The job's executor.** Every step is accomplished by the executor of the job. A step that someone else accomplishes
  is part of their job: a related job in the topology. The executor may depend on others (the board's approval arrives);
  the step is then what the executor must accomplish about it (*confirm the transfer has been approved*).
- **One level.** A job map breaks a job into steps, not steps into sub-steps. A step that needs its own breakdown is a
  sign the job was stated too high, or that the step is really a job.

## Execution seams

Where a step's execution happens outside any system (the negotiation, the payment, the signature, the court order), say
so on the step: whether it **happens in the world** (the executor does it, but not through any system) or **arrives
from the world** (the executor receives its result). The map is then honest about what software could support or
perform, and what it can only receive.

## A step is not a glossary activity

A step is an analytical construct: a milestone in getting this job done. A glossary activity (*Audit*, *Settlement*,
*Allotment*) is an act the domain itself names and talks about. One step may involve several such activities; one
activity may recur across several steps and several jobs. Do not copy the map into the glossary as activities, or the
glossary's activities into the map as steps. A step's nouns should be glossary terms; its verb is the executor's.

## Errors to watch for

- **A process map in disguise.** Steps that name today's documents, systems and hand-offs. Ask what each is for.
- **A step per type.** Eight steps, one per type, several of them thin. The types are questions, not slots.
- **A related job's steps in the map.** The auditor's verification appearing as a Monitor step. Different executor;
  back to the topology.
- **Steps that are glossary activities transcribed.** *Allot* as a step because the glossary has *Allotment*.
- **Sub-steps.** A step broken into steps of its own: the job is too high, or the step is a job.
- **Forty steps.** The job was stated too high, or the map has absorbed its adjacent jobs.
- **Seams left unmarked.** A map that reads as though a system did everything.
